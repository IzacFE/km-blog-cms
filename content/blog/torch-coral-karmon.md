The torch coral (*Euphyllia glabrescens*) is a large-polyp stony (LPS) coral with long, flowing tentacles that end in rounded tips. In Indonesia many reefers call it **karmon**, short for "karang anemon" (anemone coral), because its swaying tentacles look a lot like an anemone. Some clownfish even host in it.

## The Euphyllia Family

Torch corals belong to a popular group of LPS corals:

- **Torch (*Euphyllia glabrescens*)**: long tentacles with round tips
- **Hammer**: tentacle tips shaped like a hammer or anchor
- **Frogspawn**: tentacles that split into small branches

Hammer and frogspawn are now often placed in the genus *Fimbriaphyllia*, but most reefers still group all three as "Euphyllia". All three grow from hard skeletons, either as single heads or as branching colonies.

## Care Requirements

### Lighting
- Moderate light, around PAR 75–150
- Too much light can make the tentacles shrink and the colour fade

### Water Flow
- Moderate, gentle flow
- The tentacles should sway, not get pushed flat or torn

### Water Parameters
- Temperature: 24–26°C (75–79°F)
- Salinity: 1.025–1.026 SG (34–35 ppt)
- Alkalinity: 8–10 dKH, and keep it stable
- Calcium: 400–450 ppm
- Magnesium: 1300–1400 ppm

Torch corals build a skeleton, so they use alkalinity and calcium. Big swings in alkalinity are a common cause of tissue loss. Use the [Dosing Calculator](/calculators/dosing) to make small, steady corrections.

## Give It Space

Torch corals are aggressive. At night, or when they feel crowded, they extend **sweeper tentacles** that can reach well beyond their normal size and sting nearby corals. Leave at least 15 cm of space around a torch, and more between it and other aggressive corals. It usually gets along with other Euphyllia, but watch them when they first meet.

## Feeding

Torches get most of their energy from light, but they also take food:
- Small meaty foods such as mysis or finely chopped seafood
- Feed gently with a pipette once or twice a week
- Turn the flow down briefly so the food stays on the tentacles

## Common Problems

- **Brown jelly**: a fast-spreading brown slime on the tissue. Remove the affected head quickly and dip the coral.
- **Euphyllia-eating flatworms**: small pests that eat the tissue. Dip and quarantine new Euphyllia before adding them.
- **Tentacles not extending**: check light, flow, and alkalinity, and look for corals stinging it.

## Conclusion

Karmon is one of the most beautiful LPS corals you can keep, with constant movement that makes a reef feel alive. Give it moderate light and flow, keep alkalinity stable, leave plenty of space, and quarantine new pieces, and it will grow into a stunning colony.
