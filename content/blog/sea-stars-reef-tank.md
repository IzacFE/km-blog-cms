Sea stars are some of the most recognisable animals on the reef, and a bright star on the rockwork looks great. But they are not all equal: some are reef safe and useful, some eat corals, and some slowly starve in all but the most mature tanks. Choosing the right species matters more than anything else.

## How Sea Stars Live

Sea stars move on hundreds of tiny tube feet and many feed by pushing their stomach out over their food. They have no gills or kidneys like fish, so they are very sensitive to sudden changes in salinity, temperature, and water chemistry.

## Reef-Safe Choices

- ***Fromia* stars**: small, colourful, and reef safe. They graze biofilm and need a mature tank with plenty of live rock.
- ***Linckia* stars (e.g. the blue Linckia)**: beautiful but delicate. They often starve or decline after shipping, so only add one to a large, well-established tank.
- **Sand-sifting stars (*Archaster*, *Astropecten*)**: they stir the sand bed, but they also eat the small life in it. In a small tank they can run out of food.

## Stars to Avoid in a Reef

- **Chocolate chip star (*Protoreaster nodosus*)**: eats corals, clams, and other invertebrates
- **Crown-of-thorns (*Acanthaster*)**: a coral predator, never for a reef tank
- **Green brittle star (*Ophiarachna incrassata*)**: can catch and eat small fish

Small *Asterina* stars often arrive as hitchhikers on live rock. Most are harmless, but some types nibble on corals and zoanthids. Watch them, and remove them if you see damage.

## Acclimation

This is where most sea stars are lost:
- Drip acclimate slowly, over 1–2 hours or longer
- Match salinity and temperature closely before release
- Handle it gently and keep the transfer out of water as short as possible

## Care Requirements

- Temperature: 24–26°C (75–79°F)
- Salinity: very stable, 1.025–1.026 SG (34–35 ppt)
- A mature tank, ideally at least 6 months old, with lots of live rock
- Copper is deadly to sea stars, so never add it to a tank that has one

## Feeding

Most reef-safe stars graze on the film that grows on rock and glass. In smaller or younger tanks, supplement them:
- Place a small piece of shrimp or a sinking pellet under the star
- Feed a few times a week and remove leftovers

## Warning Signs

- Not moving for many days
- White patches, lesions, or tissue falling apart
- Arms curling up or breaking off

These are usually signs of starvation or water quality problems. Act quickly by checking your parameters and offering food.

## Conclusion

A sea star can be a great addition to a reef, as long as you pick a reef-safe species, acclimate it slowly, and add it to a mature tank. Do your research before you buy, because many stars that look perfect in the shop do not survive long at home.
